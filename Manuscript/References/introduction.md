High-throughput sequencing (HTS), also referred to as next-generation sequencing (NGS), is increasingly viewed as a powerful platform for adventitious agent detection because it is not limited to predefined targets and can therefore detect both expected and unexpected viral sequences in complex biological materials. In contrast to targeted PCR assays, HTS offers broad genomic scope and the potential to detect divergent or previously uncharacterized agents. However, its value in biologics safety testing depends not simply on whether viral reads can be generated, but on whether a low-level signal can be interpreted reliably across different matrices, virus classes, workflow designs, and laboratories. For this reason, current validation efforts increasingly frame HTS as an end-to-end analytical workflow rather than a standalone sequencing step, with overall performance determined jointly by sample preparation, sequencing, bioinformatic analysis, and predefined interpretation rules.[1-3]

A central analytical challenge is the risk of false-positive detection arising from contamination, cross-talk, and index misassignment. Because HTS is highly sensitive, contaminating nucleic acids from reagents, consumables, laboratory surfaces, carryover from high-titer samples, or multiplexed sequencing runs may be detected even when they do not represent true contamination of the test article. This problem is amplified in low-signal applications, where a small number of artifactual reads may satisfy a permissive positivity rule. In multiplexed sequencing, index hopping and sample-index misassignment can generate apparent low-level positives by transferring reads between pooled libraries, particularly on patterned-flow-cell platforms using exclusion amplification chemistry.[4,5] In the biologics context, HTS may also detect biologically irrelevant viral nucleic acid carryover, such as inert sequences introduced from reagents or raw materials, rather than evidence of replicating virus. This limitation has motivated approaches such as sequencing newly synthesized RNA to distinguish active infection from passive sequence carryover.[6] Consequently, contamination-aware controls, extraction blanks, no-template controls, physically separated workflow areas, careful multiplex design, and nonredundant dual indexing are not merely procedural refinements, but core requirements for assay credibility.[1,4-6]

The corresponding risk of false-negative detection is equally important. A negative HTS result does not necessarily imply the absence of adventitious virus, because authentic viral signal can be lost or missed at multiple points in the workflow. Upstream, inefficient or selective extraction may reduce recovery of particular virus classes depending on genome type, strandedness, size, secondary structure, or whether the virus is enveloped. Pretreatments intended to enrich viral nucleic acid, such as nuclease digestion or selective RNA- or DNA-focused processing, may inadvertently reduce recovery of fragile, damaged, partially degraded, or non-encapsidated genomes. Downstream, abundant host or product-related background may suppress viral signal below the practical LoD, and insufficient sequencing depth may leave low-frequency viral fragments unsampled. Additional bias may be introduced during reverse transcription, second-strand synthesis, amplification, fragmentation, and library preparation, all of which can create uneven genome representation and reduce detectability for some viruses. False negatives may also be introduced computationally, for example through aggressive host subtraction, stringent low-complexity filtering, restrictive alignment parameters, excessive contig-length requirements, or incomplete reference databases that fail to recognize genetically divergent viruses. Accordingly, the LoD in HTS-based adventitious-agent testing should be understood not as a fixed instrument property, but as a conditional feature of a specific workflow applied to a defined matrix and analyte class.[1-3,12,13]

A further major source of analytical uncertainty lies in bioinformatic interpretation and reference-database quality. Viral read classification can be confounded by sequencing artifacts, adapter or vector remnants, low-complexity sequence, host-derived reads that survive subtraction, and conserved regions shared among related viruses or between viruses and host genomes. Public reference collections may also contain contaminated records, taxonomic misannotation, fragmented entries, incomplete genomes, and reference sequences that include nonviral content. These issues are particularly consequential in adventitious-agent testing because positivity may depend on a small number of reads, and a few misassigned alignments can therefore appear disproportionately important.[7-9] For that reason, robust pipelines usually incorporate host subtraction, low-complexity masking, quality trimming, curated or version-locked viral references, and secondary confirmation against broader databases to test whether a candidate viral read is better explained by a nonviral source. The development and ongoing refinement of the Reference Viral Database (RVDB) reflects this need, with explicit efforts to reduce nonviral content, remove problematic entries, and improve the specificity and interpretability of HTS-based virus detection.[9,10]

This point is reflected in the growing emphasis on matrix-specific LoD validation. In HTS-based assays, the LoD is shaped by the amount of viral nucleic acid entering the workflow, extraction efficiency, reverse-transcription and library-conversion losses, sequencing depth, host-background burden, and intrinsic properties of the virus itself. Published biologics-focused studies have shown that HTS can detect diverse model viruses at approximately 10^3-10^4 genome copies per mL in some matrices, but also that sensitivity varies materially across virus types and across laboratories using different workflows.[2,3,12,13] Thus, a single platform-wide LoD is difficult to justify. Instead, LoD should be established using replicate spike-recovery studies in relevant matrices, with representative virus panels spanning RNA and DNA viruses, enveloped and non-enveloped viruses, and genomes of differing size and structure. Recent conference and multi-laboratory studies have reinforced that sensitivity claims should be linked to the validated end-to-end workflow, and that modifications to extraction chemistry, library preparation, sequencing conditions, or bioinformatic processing may necessitate bridging or revalidation.[2,3,12,13]

An additional false-negative mechanism arises from redundancy within the reference genome collection itself. When a database contains numerous highly similar contigs, such as isolate-specific or substrain-specific genomes from the same virus species, sequencing reads may align with similar scores to multiple references. Under those conditions, support for a true virus may be distributed across several related contigs rather than concentrated on a single accession. If fixed thresholds are then applied per contig or per accession, each reference may appear to have only weak support even though the aggregate evidence for the taxon is substantial. By contrast, when the same reads are remapped to a single representative genome, the aligner may accept a modest mismatch penalty caused by natural sequence divergence and consolidate the evidence, thereby increasing read count and breadth of coverage for that genome. This means that broad database screening and single-reference confirmation are not analytically equivalent steps, and the use of identical thresholds across both stages can introduce false-negative bias. The rationale for clustered or reduced-redundancy viral databases, as well as abundance-aware read reassignment methods for highly similar strains, is consistent with this problem.[9-11]

These considerations directly affect calling thresholds and the question of semi-quantitative or quantitative interpretation. In practice, HTS-based adventitious-agent workflows are generally better regarded as qualitative detection assays with predefined positivity criteria rather than as intrinsically quantitative platforms. A raw read-count threshold alone is rarely sufficient, because read abundance is influenced by extraction recovery, amplification bias, genome length, sequence divergence, background competition, and database structure. Contemporary workflows therefore tend to combine several criteria, such as minimum read support, aligned read length, contig support, breadth of genomic coverage, taxonomic consistency, comparison with negative controls, and exclusion of low-complexity or nonspecific matches.[2,3,12] Within a fixed and validated workflow, normalized read counts may provide a semi-quantitative indication of relative burden, especially across dilution series or controlled spike studies, but full quantification is substantially harder to justify without external calibrators and orthogonal anchoring such as qPCR or dPCR. For regulated adventitious-agent testing, it is therefore more accurate to describe HTS as a qualitative assay with defined detection thresholds and, at most, limited semi-quantitative interpretive value unless additional quantitative validation has been performed.[2,3,12]

Finally, variability introduced by different standard operating procedures (SOPs) remains a major barrier to comparability across laboratories. Extraction methods differ in their recovery of RNA versus DNA viruses and of enveloped versus non-enveloped particles; nuclease pretreatment can enrich encapsidated viral genomes while also suppressing some true signal; and reverse-transcription chemistry, amplification strategy, library-preparation kit, indexing method, sequencing instrument, and software versions can each alter the final distribution of reads. Studies in metagenomics more broadly have shown that library preparation and sequencing platform introduce systematic bias, while interlaboratory comparisons have demonstrated that protocol differences materially affect reproducibility.[14,15] Biologics-focused HTS studies now show the same general pattern: laboratories using different extraction chemistries, library-preparation procedures, sequencing platforms, and bioinformatic pipelines can detect the same viruses with different apparent sensitivity and specificity.[3,12,13] Taken together, these observations suggest that successful implementation of HTS for adventitious-agent detection requires not only analytical sensitivity, but also rigorous standardization, modular validation, predefined controls, stage-specific calling criteria, and careful bridging whenever any part of the workflow is changed.[1,3,12-15]
