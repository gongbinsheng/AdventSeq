# AdVentSeq: A Comprehensive Bioinformatics Pipeline for Adventitious Viral Agents Detection with RNA-seq

## Abstract

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is emerging as a powerful agnostic tool for detecting adventitious viral agents in biologics. NGS of virus-infected host samples provides powerful insights into host–pathogen interactions. However, NGS requires advanced computational methodologies for accurate interpretation. Existing bioinformatics tools often lack the ability to simultaneously achieve comprehensive pathogen characterization and precise quantification, presenting a critical gap for regulatory science applications where systematic evaluation of NGS methods is essential to define best practices.

We present AdVentSeq, a robust bioinformatics pipeline designed for paired-end short-read RNA sequencing data from virus-infected host systems. AdVentSeq enables integrated analysis of both host transcriptional responses and viral genomic features. The pipeline incorporates rigorous quality control, adapter trimming and read filtering, followed by host genome alignment to support host expression profiling and removal of host-derived reads. Non-host reads undergo alignment against FDA’s Reference Viral Database (RVDB), enabling genome coverage analysis to identify candidate viral infections. A subsequent refinement step performs combined viral genome remapping with coverage-adjusted quantification, improving accuracy of pathogen detection. The workflow also generates downstream-ready outputs for applications such as viral variant calling, phylogenetic analysis, and host-response characterization, although those analyses were not the focus of the present study.

Benchmarking across multiple library-preparation protocols and inoculum conditions showed that host-subtraction strategy strongly influenced downstream viral candidate inflation and apparent viral burden. Conservative host read removal reduced spurious carryover into the viral-analysis stage, while coverage-aware quantification helped distinguish localized mapping artifacts from broad genome-supported viral signal. Host-expression profiles remained broadly consistent across host mappers, supporting a practical separation between mapper choices optimized for host transcriptomics and those optimized for viral detection and quantification.

By integrating host transcriptomic profiling with pathogen detection, quantification, and genomic characterization, AdVentSeq offers a comprehensive solution tailored to regulatory science needs. Overall, our results support a workflow in which conservative host read removal is paired with coverage-aware viral interpretation to improve robustness, transparency, and biological plausibility in RNA-seq-based adventitious agent detection. Its application is particularly relevant for FDA safety evaluations of biological products, supporting systematic identification and characterization of adventitious viral agents.

**Keywords:** RNA-seq, viral genomics, bioinformatics pipeline, pathogen detection, host-pathogen interactions

## Introduction

Ensuring the viral safety of biological products is a central requirement of modern biomanufacturing because adventitious viral contamination can affect cell substrates, raw materials, viral seeds, bulk harvests, and final products used in vaccines, recombinant proteins, cell therapies, and gene therapy applications (32660862, 36543633, 32094072, 37478506). The regulatory and manufacturing importance of this problem has driven the development of multilayered viral safety strategies that combine careful selection and qualification of starting materials, upstream process controls, orthogonal clearance steps, and sensitive analytical testing for known and unknown viral agents (32660862, 36543633, 32094072, 30332784).

Historically, adventitious virus testing has relied on *in vivo* assays, *in vitro* cell-based infectivity tests, electron microscopy, and targeted molecular methods such as PCR, each of which provides useful but incomplete coverage of viral risk (32660862, 36543633, 32094072). These traditional assays are limited by long turnaround times, finite assay scope, dependence on viral replication or prior sequence knowledge, and reduced ability to detect divergent, unexpected, or previously uncharacterized viruses (32660862, 36543633, 40945333).

Next-generation sequencing (NGS), also referred to as high-throughput sequencing (HTS), has therefore emerged as a powerful sequence-independent technology for broad virus detection and characterization in biologics safety testing workflows (32660862, 40945333, 37478506, 40179635). Because NGS does not require prior knowledge of a specific target, it offers the possibility of simultaneously detecting expected, unexpected, and novel viral sequences within a single assay, making it particularly attractive for comprehensive adventitious agent testing (32660862, 40945333, 37478506). The relevance of NGS-based virus detection was underscored by prominent case studies, including the detection of porcine circovirus type 1 nucleic acid in a licensed rotavirus vaccine and the discovery of an unexpected rhabdovirus in the Sf9 insect cell line, both of which demonstrated the value of broad, untargeted sequencing strategies in revealing hidden viral signals (32660862, 30332784). These examples also highlighted a broader lesson for the field: contamination and viral sequence findings that escape conventional assays may still be detectable when sequencing data are interpreted using appropriately designed laboratory and computational workflows (30332784, 37478506, 40179635). As a result, NGS is increasingly being adopted as a complementary or alternative method for viral safety testing in biologics manufacturing, including applications involving vaccines, recombinant biologics, cell substrates, and viral vector platforms (32094072, 32660862, 37478506, 40179635, 40945333).

However, the analytical power of NGS depends not only on sequencing chemistry and library preparation, but also on the design, optimization, and validation of the downstream bioinformatics pipeline used to separate true viral signal from technical and biological background (30093175, 37478506, 40945333). Bioinformatics analysis is especially consequential in low-titer or complex samples, where viral reads may represent only a minute fraction of total sequence output and where false-positive calls can arise from database contamination, read misclassification, endogenous viral elements, or spurious alignments to repetitive or low-complexity sequence (30093175, 30262776, 40945333). Accordingly, several groups and collaborative working efforts have emphasized that pipeline benchmarking, reference curation, and threshold setting are essential for generating reproducible and interpretable NGS-based virus detection results in regulated settings (30093175, 37478506, 40945333, 40179635). Among the different NGS applications relevant to viral safety and host–virus biology, RNA-seq of virus-infected host samples is particularly informative because the same dataset can capture viral RNA, host transcriptional responses, and, when coverage is sufficient, sequence variation within the infecting viral population (28103897, 29976658, 33017003, 29922260). This dual utility makes infected-host RNA-seq attractive not only for basic studies of virus–host interaction, but also for translational and industrial settings in which one wishes to understand both the presence of viral material and the corresponding impact on host-cell state (28103897, 33017003, 37468389). In contrast to DNA-based broad-range sequencing approaches that may detect residual or nonreplicating nucleic acid, transcriptome sequencing can provide evidence of transcriptionally active virus while simultaneously preserving information about host pathways perturbed during infection (37468389, 32660862, 28103897). For infected-cell systems, this creates an opportunity to connect viral burden with host innate immune activation, stress responses, metabolism, cell-cycle changes, or other transcriptional phenotypes within a unified analytical framework (28103897, 29976658, 33017003). Despite these advantages, extracting accurate viral abundance estimates from infected-host RNA-seq data remains challenging because host reads usually dominate the library and can obscure low-level viral signal (30262776, 30332784, 28103897).

The host background problem is compounded when viral sequences share partial similarity with host-derived transcripts or genomic regions, which can lead to ambiguous alignment, incomplete recovery of true viral reads, or erroneous reassignment of reads during host subtraction and downstream classification (30262776, 30093175). Method-dependent host read subtraction is itself a major analytical variable because subtraction against the host genome or transcriptome can improve computational efficiency and reduce noise, yet overly aggressive filtering may also discard biologically meaningful viral reads, particularly for viruses with host-like, endogenous, or low-complexity sequence features (30262776, 30093175). Another recurring challenge is that viral reads are often distributed unevenly across the viral genome because of transcriptional structure, library construction bias, PCR effects, partial degradation, and local sequence composition, making raw read counts an imperfect proxy for viral abundance (33017003, 29922260). Uneven coverage becomes especially problematic when a small number of highly covered regions dominate the apparent signal while other parts of the genome remain sparsely represented, since such patterns can inflate abundance estimates or complicate comparisons across viruses, samples, and analytical methods (29922260, 33017003). Low-complexity or homopolymeric regions can further distort interpretation because they tend to attract spurious alignments, generate unstable read-placement behavior across mappers, and create localized coverage artifacts that are not necessarily proportional to true viral load (30093175, 29922260). Multi-mapping introduces an additional layer of complexity because reads may align to multiple viral references, to both host and viral references, or to closely related strains within a database, thereby complicating taxonomic assignment and quantitative inference (30262776, 40945333, 29922260). These issues are amplified by differences among aligners and classifiers, since distinct tools vary in sensitivity, specificity, handling of mismatches and indels, treatment of multi-mapped reads, and susceptibility to misleading low-complexity matches (30093175, 40945333, 29922260). Reference strategy is likewise critical, as viral detection and quantification can change depending on whether one uses broad taxonomic databases, curated virus-only references, host-plus-virus composite references, or strain-resolved panels tailored to the experimental system (30093175, 30262776, 40945333).

Taken together, these factors mean that infected-host RNA-seq analysis requires more than simple read counting or off-the-shelf metagenomic classification if one aims to obtain robust viral quantification alongside interpretable host-response results (28103897, 33017003, 29922260). Yet many existing pipelines emphasize only one side of the problem: some focus primarily on metagenomic virus identification and broad taxonomic screening, while others center on host differential expression without providing dedicated support for viral quantification or viral sequence analysis after infection (30262776, 33017003, 29922260). Comparatively fewer workflows provide an integrated approach that supports host gene-expression quantification, virus-aware abundance estimation, and post-infection viral mutation analysis while explicitly accounting for alignment ambiguity, coverage heterogeneity, and host-subtraction artifacts (33017003, 29922260). This gap is especially relevant for controlled experimental infection studies and spike-in systems, where the analytical objective is not merely to detect whether virus is present, but to quantify how much virus is present, characterize how the host responds, and determine whether viral sequence changes emerge under the tested conditions (28103897, 29976658, 33017003). Such integrated analysis is also valuable for benchmarking purposes because controlled mixtures and spike-in designs can reveal where specific tools succeed or fail under conditions of known composition, read imbalance, and varying degrees of host background (37478506, 40179635, 40945333).

In this study, we address these needs by developing AdVentSeq, a comprehensive bioinformatics pipeline for Illumina paired-end RNA-seq data from virus-infected host samples, together with ViraQuant, a viral quantification framework designed to improve abundance estimation in the presence of uneven coverage and alignment artifacts. AdVentSeq is intended to support end-to-end analysis by combining viral detection, coverage-aware viral quantification, host transcriptional profiling, and post-infection viral mutation analysis within a single workflow tailored to infected-host transcriptomic data rather than generic metagenomic sequencing alone. Because mapper choice and reference composition can strongly influence both sensitivity and quantitative accuracy, we further emphasize systematic benchmarking of alignment tools and comparison of reference strategies as core components of the framework rather than as incidental implementation details (30093175, 30262776, 40945333). To evaluate performance under controlled conditions, we leverage a controlled multi-virus spike-in dataset that enables direct comparison of viral detection and quantification behavior across tools, references, and analytical settings while preserving the infected-host RNA-seq context relevant to downstream host-response analysis. By integrating host and viral analyses in a common framework and by explicitly confronting the sources of bias that complicate infected-host RNA-seq interpretation, AdVentSeq and ViraQuant aim to provide a practical and transparent solution for studying virus–host interactions and for improving the analytical rigor of sequencing-based viral characterization workflows (30093175, 37478506, 40945333).

## Results

### AdVentSeq workflow scope and benchmark dataset

We developed AdVentSeq as a workflow for infected-host RNA-seq that jointly evaluates viral and host-derived information from the same sequencing dataset. The pipeline includes several key steps, including quality control (QC), host/virus read separation, host gene-expression quantification, taxonomic screening, viral quantification and coverage analysis. The worflow generates downstream-ready data for host-response analysis and viral evolution analysis (Figure 1). This design was motivated by the need to move beyond pipelines that emphasize either virus detection alone or host transcriptomics alone, and instead provide a unified framework for interpreting viral support together with host transcriptional responses.

**Figure 1: AdVentSeq workflow diagram **

The QC includes QC assessment, read filtering, and adapter trimming. Optionally, rRNA reads can be removed according to the study interests. In the host/virus read separation, the quality reads are first mapped to host genome and host gene expression can be quantified. The host gene expression profile can be used for host response analyses, such as differential expressed gene analysis, function and pathway enrichment analysis, etc. The proper host mapped reads are then removed to create host-unmapped reads data. This data then undergoes through an optimal taxonomic screening for non-target analysis concluding with a list of virus candidates. Taxonomic screening can be done with either Kraken2, or with mapping to RVDB followed by coverage analysis using `ViraQuant.py`. The mapped reads can be seperated per virus using `taxonomic_classifier.py` to created BAM files for visualization and expert inspection. The targeted analysis involves mapping the Host-unmapped reads to a combined genome of know list of viruses, optionally combined with the virus candidates from the taxonomic screening results. A simple read counting is performed by `taxonomic_classifier.py`. Comprehensive coverage analysis and quantification can be done with `ViraQuant.py`. The `taxonomic_classifier.py` also creates BAM files for the virus mapped reads that can be used for evolutionary analysis. For exploration purpose, De novo assembly can be applied to the reads that are unmapped to either host or viruses  to create contigs to search for possible species/strain/isolation through BLAST. For detailed tool information, please see Materials and Methods.

The benchmark dataset (See Materials and Methods) covered 42 included human-cell RNA-seq libraries spanning seven library-preparation protocols, three inoculum groups (Control, 1:1, and 10:1), and two technical replicates per group (Table 1). In 1:1, and 10:1 groups, a mixture of seven viruses was introduced in the human cell lines at different dilution ratio. Median post-filtering sequencing depth varied widely by protocol, ranging from 95.3 million reads for RepliG+Nextera_XT libraries to 274.5 million reads for NEB\_WO\_RD libraries.

### Host subtraction dominated the amount of sequence entering the viral stage

The clearest method effect emerged before viral alignment. Across the seven protocols, BWA-MEM host subtraction left median non-host read pools of 3.4 x 10^5^ to 1.8 x 10^6^ reads, whereas HISAT2 left 7.0 x 10^6^ to 5.7 x 10^7^ reads and STAR left 2.0 x 10^6^ to 3.7 x 10^7^ reads (Figure 2A; Table 1). Relative to BWA-MEM, HISAT2 passed 13.1- to 57.2-fold more reads into the viral stage and STAR passed 4.2- to 44.3-fold more. Thus, the choice of host mapper strongly determined how much ambiguous host-like sequence was allowed to propagate into downstream viral analyses.

This carryover effect translated directly into untargeted candidate inflation. RVDBv31 screens following BWA-MEM host subtraction typically returned hundreds of taxa, whereas HISAT2- and STAR-based subtraction often yielded thousands to more than ten thousand taxa, especially when BWA-MEM was also used on the viral side (Figure 2B; Table 2). Median untargeted taxon counts in control libraries ranged from 429 to 909 after BWA-MEM host subtraction, compared with 1,295 to 8,879 after HISAT2 or STAR host subtraction. Agreement between Kraken2 and RVDB was also higher after BWA-MEM host subtraction (median shared RVDB fraction 0.441 to 0.446) than after HISAT2 or STAR host subtraction (0.158 to 0.208), indicating that permissive host subtraction broadened the candidate space more than it sharpened consensus across discovery methods (Table 2).

### Targeted viral burden was most conservative after BWA-MEM host subtraction

Panel-mode alignment to the targeted virus bundle retained clear viral signal in positive titration libraries, but the separation between controls and positives depended strongly on host subtraction strategy (Figure 3; Table 2). With BWA-MEM as the host mapper, median control-sample burden was 1.93 normalized units with Bowtie2 and 0.51 with BWA-MEM, compared with 37.49 and 36.90 in positive titration libraries. After HISAT2 or STAR host subtraction, control-sample burden increased substantially: 24.67 and 33.13 with Bowtie2 viral mapping, and 205.42 and 235.33 with BWA-MEM viral mapping. The median number of viruses detected in control libraries dropped to 2.0 with the BWA-MEM/BWA-MEM combination, whereas HISAT2- and STAR-based subtraction generally reported 6.0 to 7.5 viruses even in controls. Positive titration libraries still showed median detection of 6 to 8 viruses across the mapper combinations, indicating that conservative host subtraction reduced background much more than it reduced expected panel recovery.

### Coverage breadth distinguished localized pileups from genome-wide support

Mapped-read totals alone did not reliably reflect biologically plausible support. Sample `1_S1` provided a representative contrast between narrow and broad viral evidence (Figure 4A-B; Table 3). Under HISAT2/BWA-MEM and STAR/BWA-MEM, HEV accumulated 19,328 and 21,847 mapped reads, respectively, exceeding the corresponding Zika counts of 2,031 and 2,027 reads. However, HEV breadth remained only 3.49% to 3.53% and never passed the ViraQuant 90%-breadth criterion, whereas Zika covered 95.38% to 99.15% of positions and passed the 90%-breadth criterion under every mapper combination. Even the more permissive HISAT2/Bowtie2 and STAR/Bowtie2 combinations showed the same contrast: HEV breadth remained below 4.4%, while Zika breadth remained near complete. These results show why raw read counts can be misleading when a small genomic interval absorbs many alignments, and why breadth-style filters are required alongside depth summaries.

### ViraQuant preserved overall virus ranking while exposing coverage quality

At the virus level, ViraQuant remained strongly concordant with read-count-based summaries despite operating on a different quantitative scale. Across 336 matched virus-level observations per host/virus mapper combination, Spearman correlation between read-count and ViraQuant summaries ranged from 0.824 to 0.972 (Suppl Table 2). The highest concordance was observed for the BWA-MEM/BWA-MEM workflow, whereas the HISAT2/BWA-MEM and STAR/BWA-MEM combinations showed lower agreement, consistent with the greater opportunity for carryover-driven pileups under permissive host subtraction. In practice, these results support using `mean_depth_ge_1` as a quantitative descriptor only when it is interpreted jointly with coverage breadth metrics such as `frac_ge_1` or `pass_k_90pct_ge_1`.

### Host-expression structure was stable across host mappers

Host-side quantification was much less sensitive to mapper choice than downstream viral carryover. Pairwise mapper concordance for count-pair matrices was high across the 42 analyzed libraries, with median sample-wise Spearman correlations of 0.986 between HISAT2 and STAR, 0.938 between BWA-MEM and HISAT2, and 0.934 between BWA-MEM and STAR (Suppl Table 1). BWA-MEM yielded the largest median number of detected genes (28,909), whereas HISAT2 and STAR produced similar medians of about 26,100 genes. Despite those count differences, the overall host-expression topology was preserved across all three mappers: samples clustered primarily by library-preparation protocol, while Control, 1:1, and 10:1 libraries separated within protocols along the principal components (Suppl Figure 1).

### Recommended configuration from the benchmark

Taken together, the benchmark supported a split configuration rather than a single universal mapper. For viral inference, the most conservative workflow combined BWA-MEM host subtraction with targeted viral alignment by BWA-MEM or Bowtie2, followed by ViraQuant review using `mean_depth_ge_1` together with breadth-style presence filters. For host-transcriptome-focused analyses, splice-aware mappers remained appropriate because host-expression structure was highly consistent across mappers and HISAT2/STAR agreed most closely with one another. AdVentSeq also produces downstream-ready outputs for host-response, variant-calling, and phylogenetic follow-up, but those analyses were outside the scope of the present benchmark and are therefore not interpreted further here.

## Discussion

Infected-host RNA-seq is an appealing substrate for virus analysis because it can, in principle, support several objectives at once: detection of viral sequences, estimation of viral abundance, characterization of host transcriptional responses, and assessment of viral sequence variation. In practice, however, these objectives place different demands on the computational workflow, and our results show that methods that appear favorable for one task are not necessarily optimal for another. The central finding of this study is that viral quantification in infected-host RNA-seq is especially vulnerable to coverage artifacts and alignment ambiguity, and that reliable interpretation therefore requires a coverage-aware strategy rather than naïve counting of mapped reads alone.

A major reason naïve quantification fails is that viral reads are often distributed unevenly across reference genomes. Uneven coverage can arise from several sources simultaneously. First, some viral regions share local similarity with host-derived sequences, including repetitive elements, simple sequence tracts, or other compositionally biased regions, which can cause ambiguous placement of reads during host subtraction or viral alignment. Second, low-complexity and homopolymer-rich regions can attract spurious alignments because a read may match multiple loci with similarly weak specificity. Third, the structure of RNA-seq libraries themselves can introduce nonuniform representation through fragmentation bias, PCR amplification bias, transcript abundance differences, RNA degradation, and positional effects. In infected-host datasets, these issues are magnified because viral reads are typically sparse relative to host reads. As a result, a small cluster of ambiguously aligned reads in one short viral interval can create the appearance of a positive signal even when broader genome support is absent. Under those conditions, total mapped read count becomes a misleading summary, because it gives disproportionate weight to localized pileups that may reflect alignment artifacts rather than true viral abundance.

This is precisely why breadth-aware and depth-distribution-aware quantification is important. A robust viral signal should generally be supported across a meaningful fraction of the viral reference rather than collapsing onto one narrow region. Measures such as the fraction of positions with nonzero depth, the mean depth among covered positions, and coverage quantiles provide a more informative description of evidence than raw read totals. These metrics do not eliminate ambiguity entirely, but they reduce the risk of over-interpreting isolated high-depth artifacts. In this respect, ViraQuant is less a replacement for read counting than a correction to the assumption that all mapped reads contribute equally to biological interpretation. Our results suggest that once uneven coverage is acknowledged explicitly, apparent differences in viral support can be re-evaluated more realistically.

The benchmarking results also support an important practical conclusion: splitting analytical objectives across tools is rational rather than inconsistent. In many workflows there is an implicit desire to identify a single “best” mapper, but infected-host RNA-seq does not present a single optimization problem. Host transcriptomic profiling benefits from splice-aware alignment and mature gene-quantification support, which makes tools such as STAR or HISAT2 well suited for host-side analysis. Viral quantification, by contrast, places greater emphasis on conservative read assignment, minimization of spurious recovery after host subtraction, and transparent inspection of coverage patterns. Our results indicate that BWA-MEM is advantageous in this context because it can be used more conservatively for host subtraction and viral realignment, reducing some of the misleading signal retained by more permissive strategies. This division of labor is methodologically justified: one tool need not be forced to perform optimally for every downstream objective when the objectives themselves differ in what they reward. Using STAR for host transcriptomics and BWA-MEM for subtraction and viral quantification is therefore not a compromise, but a recognition that integrated host–virus analysis is inherently multi-objective.

These findings also have implications for how users should think about “discovery mode” versus “panel mode” workflows. Discovery-mode analysis aims to cast a wide net across diverse viral space, often using broad reference databases or metagenomic classifiers. The advantage is obvious: unexpected or divergent viral signals may be captured. The drawback is that noise increases substantially as database breadth expands. Tools such as Kraken2 are highly useful for rapid taxonomic screening, but realistic expectations are essential. In complex infected-host RNA-seq data, broad k-mer-based classification can surface numerous low-level candidate taxa arising from shared sequence content, database contamination, endogenous elements, or simple compositional matches. Such tools are valuable for triage and prioritization, but they should not be treated as definitive evidence of infection without secondary validation. Panel-mode workflows, by contrast, use curated and narrower references tailored to the experimental context. They trade breadth for interpretability and are typically better suited for quantification once plausible candidate viruses are known. Our results support a staged strategy in which discovery-mode screening is followed by panel-mode confirmation and coverage-aware quantification. In other words, broad classification is best viewed as hypothesis generation, whereas quantitative interpretation should rely on more constrained references and explicit coverage review.

The current study has several limitations. First, the evaluated dataset is a controlled spike-in design, which is valuable for benchmarking because expected viral composition is known, but it does not capture the full biological and technical variability of naturally infected or manufacturing-relevant samples. Real-world specimens may contain more heterogeneous host backgrounds, broader dynamic ranges of viral burden, mixed infections, degraded RNA, and unanticipated contaminants. Second, the virus set tested here is limited in diversity. Performance observed on the evaluated panel may not generalize uniformly across all genome types, segment architectures, transcript structures, or sequence-complexity profiles. Third, the workflow remains dependent on reference completeness and curation. Any alignment-based or classification-based framework can miss viruses that are too divergent from the available references, and even known viruses may be underrepresented if references are incomplete, misannotated, or taxonomically redundant. Fourth, while AdVentSeq includes support for post-infection viral mutation analysis, mutation calling sensitivity is inherently constrained by sequencing depth, local error profiles, amplification bias, and mapping ambiguity. Low-frequency variant detection is particularly vulnerable to false positives when depth is shallow or concentrated in problematic regions, and any conclusions about viral evolution must therefore be interpreted in the context of site-level coverage and platform-specific error characteristics.

These limitations point directly to several areas for future work. One priority is explicit low-complexity masking or annotation, either at the reference level or during post-alignment filtering, to reduce the influence of homopolymer-driven artifacts. Relatedly, the use of decoy sequences could improve subtraction and alignment specificity by absorbing reads that would otherwise map spuriously to viral references. Better handling of multimapping is also needed. Current strategies often reduce ambiguity to a binary keep-or-discard decision, but more nuanced probabilistic or weighting-based methods may improve quantification when reads are shared across related viruses or between host and viral sequences. Another important extension would be tighter integration of assembly-based or contig-based validation. Read-level quantification is useful, but local assembly can provide orthogonal evidence that a signal reflects coherent viral sequence rather than scattered short-read artifacts. In regulated or quality-control settings, assembly-supported confirmation may be especially persuasive for follow-up interpretation.

Standardized reporting is another important future direction. For applications relevant to biologics safety or other regulated environments, users need outputs that are transparent, reproducible, and easy to review across studies. Standardized summary tables, coverage plots, alignment evidence categories, thresholds, and audit-friendly provenance would make the framework more practical for routine use. Reporting conventions that clearly distinguish screening hits, quantifiable signals, and validated calls would also help prevent over-interpretation. More broadly, future benchmarking should extend beyond controlled spike-ins to include experimentally infected models, orthogonally validated samples, and challenging negative controls. Such work would help define where coverage-aware quantification is most robust and where additional safeguards are required.

Overall, this study argues that infected-host RNA-seq should not be analyzed with a single simplistic metric or a one-size-fits-all mapping strategy. Viral quantification, host transcriptomics, and viral sequence characterization are related but distinct goals, and integrated workflows perform best when they acknowledge those differences explicitly. By combining objective-specific alignment choices with coverage-aware viral quantification, AdVentSeq and ViraQuant move toward a more realistic and interpretable framework for host–virus RNA-seq analysis. The broader implication is that the field may benefit less from chasing a universally optimal tool and more from designing transparent, modular workflows that match method choice to analytical objective while preserving clear standards for evidence and reporting.

## Conclusion

In this study, we developed AdVentSeq as an integrated workflow for the analysis of virus-infected host RNA-seq data and introduced ViraQuant as a coverage-aware framework for viral quantification. Together, these tools address a central challenge in infected-host transcriptomic analysis: how to extract reliable viral abundance information while preserving the parallel value of the dataset for host gene-expression profiling and downstream viral sequence analysis.

Our results show that apparent viral read recovery is highly dependent on mapper choice and host-subtraction strategy, and that higher viral read counts do not necessarily correspond to more accurate viral detection. In particular, localized pileups in low-complexity or homopolymer-rich regions can create misleading viral signals, especially when read assignment is influenced by ambiguous host–virus similarity or tool-specific alignment behavior. These findings highlight that raw mapped-read totals alone are insufficient for robust interpretation of infected-host RNA-seq data.

By explicitly incorporating genome coverage breadth, depth distribution, and covered-position summaries, ViraQuant provides a more informative view of viral support than conventional count-based approaches. This coverage-adjusted strategy helps distinguish biologically plausible viral signal from artifacts driven by uneven coverage or spurious alignment, and it supports both contig-level and virus-level interpretation in complex reference settings. More broadly, AdVentSeq demonstrates that integrated analysis of viral signal and host transcriptional response is feasible within a single framework and can provide a more complete picture of virus–host interactions than workflows focused on only one side of the problem.

Taken together, AdVentSeq and ViraQuant offer a practical foundation for infected-host RNA-seq analysis in both research and applied settings, including viral characterization, benchmarking studies, and sequencing-based workflows relevant to biologics safety assessment. Future work should expand validation across larger spike-in panels, diverse host backgrounds, and experimentally confirmed infection models, and further evaluate how coverage-aware quantification relates to orthogonal viral load measurements and viral variant recovery. With these extensions, AdVentSeq has the potential to become a robust and transparent framework for integrated host–virus transcriptomic analysis.

## Materials and Methods

### Dataset Preparation

The benchmark dataset consisted of paired-end Illumina NovaSeq 6000 RNA-seq libraries from a human cell-line study distributed across seven library-preparation protocols: `Jumpcode`, `Jumpcode_Trio`, `NEB_RD`, `NEB_WO_RD`, `RepliG+Nextera_XT`, `Ribozero+NEBNext`, and `Trio`. Each included protocol contributed two Control libraries, two `1:1` libraries, and two `10:1` libraries, yielding 42 included libraries for the main comparison. Four additional `U937*` libraries were inventoried but excluded from the comparative benchmark, following the locked analysis plan.

### Evaluation of Mapping tools

Host alignment was evaluated with BWA-MEM, HISAT2, and STAR. Viral alignment in the finalized benchmark was evaluated with BWA-MEM and Bowtie2 on the read pairs that remained after host subtraction. The earlier presentation deck also reflected exploratory minimap2 examples, but the finalized `Analyses/full_run` outputs presented here were generated from the BWA-MEM and Bowtie2 viral-alignment comparison.

### Read QC and preprocessing

Raw RNA-seq reads were processed with `fastp` to generate before- and after-filtering read totals, retention fractions, and the read denominators used for downstream normalization. The manuscript figures and tables use the `after_filtering_total_reads` field as the primary total-read denominator.

### Host alignment and host gene quantification

Reads were aligned to `hg38` with each host mapper. Host gene quantification was generated from the `hg38_ncbiRefSeq_exon` featureCounts outputs, and both `count_pairs` and `count_reads` matrices were retained. The benchmark figures focus on the `count_pairs` matrices.

### Host read-pair removal

For each sample and host mapper, the primary host-removal denominator was defined from the matching `samtools` stats output as `after_filtering_total_reads - with_itself_and_mate_mapped`. This denominator quantified the read pool that remained available for viral mapping after host subtraction and was propagated to the targeted and untargeted viral summaries.

### Viral Taxonomic Screening and Alignment

Two viral-reference modes were benchmarked. In panel mode, unmapped read pairs were aligned to the legacy `7viruses` bundle, which in the finalized outputs contained eight virus groups: EBV, FeLV, HEV, PCV1, REO1, REO3, RSV, and Zika. In untargeted mode, the same read pairs were aligned to RVDBv31, and those outputs were compared with Kraken2 viral taxid reports as a measure of discovery-mode agreement.

### Coverage Analysis and Quantification (ViraQuant.py)

Coverage-aware viral summarization was performed with ViraQuant on coordinate-sorted viral BAM files. ViraQuant reports both contig-level and virus-level summaries, including mapped reads, mapped reads per base pair, `frac_ge_1`, `mean_depth_all`, `mean_depth_ge_1`, `median_depth_ge_1`, quantile-based depth summaries, and pass/fail indicators for thresholds such as `pass_k_90pct_ge_1`. Virus-level rows are produced by pooling per-base depth histograms across all contigs assigned to the same virus group.

### Advanced Analytics

The AdVentSeq workflow is intended to generate downstream-ready inputs for host-response analysis and viral mutation analysis after the alignment and quantification stages. These analyses were not in scope for the present study. Accordingly, the finalized project outputs supplied here were used to benchmark detection, subtraction, and coverage-aware quantification rather than to interpret differential-expression, pathway-enrichment, variant-calling, or phylogenetic results.
